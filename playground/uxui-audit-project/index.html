01 · the questions

Five questions — across both surfaces.

  • Who do we optimise for? Which staff segment, and which resident segment — we can't serve all equally.
  • Where does the loop break? A friction inventory across every back-office and front-office stage, tagged to a principle and to evidence.
  • Which breaks matter most? Severity × frequency × business impact — anchored to the 68 open dispatch tickets.
  • What does "good" look like? A target metric per surface, baselined now so improvement is provable.
  • Will the fixes hold up with real people? Validated with staff and with residents — especially on mobile.

02 · the framework — the structured process you liked

Twelve principles, two personas a side, scored the same way every time.

Principles & scoring

  • Nielsen's 10 + Shneiderman's closure & internal locus of control — every finding tagged to one
  • Severity 0–4 × frequency → one priority score, comparable across the whole audit
  • 3+ evaluators, then affinity-clustered — defensible, not opinion

Personas, both sides

  • Staff: Joan (high savviness) · Diane (low savviness)
  • Residents: rare participant · engaged resident · skeptic
  • Each session run twice — once per persona lens

This is the agentic-audit backbone, extended to residents — what makes findings comparable and recommendations easy to defend in the room.

Hypothesis-led · prototype-as-probe

We have a point of view — and
a plan to be wrong fast.

The big bosses the audit has to beat — priors from hundreds of admin conversations and live resident sessions, one Irene's architecture map surfaced, and three the live walk turned up by triangulating the real product against the 2026 support backlog. They resolve into one project to lead with: an outcome-trust loop the support data is shouting about. Held with conviction, written to be falsifiable, tested with real prototypes in front of real people — in two weeks.

Why this shape

This isn't research vs. build. It's two risks.

If we only build

Momentum decay

A head full of pattern, spent down into a redesign nobody pressure-tested — fast, and possibly confidently wrong.

If we only research

Confident wrongness

The same context hardens into a prior we stop questioning — six weeks of extraction that just ratifies the gut.

The synthesis: name the priors as falsifiable bets, then build prototypes as the instrument that tests them. We move now and stay honest — and the bets become the agenda, not the answer.

What the audit is really fighting

Five bosses — plus three the live audit turned up.

Usability fixes are the small enemies. These are the structural ones a real redesign has to beat. They line up in two pairs and a gate: an outcome-trust loop (4 + 7) — the loudest thing in the support data — and an adoption wall (2 + 5) — Irene's brief. Boss 8 is the gate the whole thing has to clear. Boss 1 and Boss 3 we hold more lightly — design and trajectory bets, not pain we can yet count.

Boss 1 · the Wall of Everything

The project page is the hub. Each method opens into its own focused, full-screen surface — never piled on a scroll.

Why I believe it

Parallel participation makes the scroll impossible — you can't stack four live methods down one page. A preview that launches a focused surface is how the novice knows what's next.

What would prove me wrong

Already half-true — and inconsistent — on the live FO: a single-method page is reasonably focused, and a native survey on the epic tenant already launches into its own full-screen surface ("Take the survey" → splash → form). But on another tenant the same method is embedded inline under "Phases," and multi-phase projects still stack navigator + method + events. So this isn't "every method on one scroll" — it's launched vs embedded, applied inconsistently. And the gate trades away serendipity: today you stumble into the map and start dropping pins.

How we probe it

One multi-phase project: build the hub + one method full-screen → test entry → focus → return with 5 residents, on their phones. The single-method case is the control. The real bet isn't "make it full-screen" — it's make launch-vs-embed consistent and legible.

Triangulation flag (round 3): this boss is observation-led, not ticket-corroborated. The 2026 dispatch backlog is almost entirely back-office; the resident-side scroll barely registers in support volume. So conviction here rests on two-tenant reproduction + the parallel-participation argument, not on user pain we can count. Treated as a design bet, and the probe is built to prove it moves resident behaviour or drop it.

Conviction medium-high · held lightly — a design bet, reproduced on two tenants but never named in the backlog; not part of the project we lead with

Boss 2 · the Vienna Paradox

Cities don't need less power. They just shouldn't see it until they do.

Why I believe it

The tool can run a Vienna-sized city — that's the moat. But the survey-only town drowns in it: 11 top-level destinations, the same setting (tags, access-rights) configured in two worlds, a survey edit six URL levels below /admin. The dispatch backlog is thick with setup overload.

What would prove me wrong

The tempting fix — let admins pre-set what to hide — is config-to-kill-config, and breeds "my admin turned it off, now I can't find it." So the bet is just-in-time disclosure by task, not a setup ritual. And if the complexity is conceptual (what even is a "phase"?), hiding controls won't be enough.

How we probe it

A survey-only fast lane that reveals advanced on demand → Diane (low-savviness) + a Vienna-scale power user.

Confirmed live on the epic tenant (June '26): four nav layers to reach method config — sidebar (11) → project tabs (6) → phase strip → eight per-phase sub-tabs. Triangulated (round 3): the config surfaces this drowns people in are exactly the dispatch backlog's busiest product hotspots — Survey Form 12 tickets (the single biggest), Registration/Demographics 9, Ideation 6 — and prior findings F02 / F15 / F16 (overload at create + method setup). Architecture from the real admin code (Irene's BO map): the complexity is the Project→Phase→Method model projected onto the screen — polish alone can't remove it; the model has to be re-surfaced, not just re-styled.

Conviction high · corroborated by tickets + findings + the architecture map

Boss 3 · the Form, in an Agent World — highest stakes

Admins will hand the complexity to an LLM. The back office becomes where they check its work.

Why I believe it

MCP-first is the trajectory — config-by-conversation (even an audio note from your phone) beats config-by-form for most cities. This eventually eats Boss 2.

What would prove me wrong

I'm an MCP power user; Diane and the publicly-accountable officer aren't — right trajectory, uncertain timing. Irene's counter-prior: simplify in-product first (templates, 1–3 clicks to 80% of the output), agents later and for power users — making this the right destination, wrong sprint. And civic config is unskimmable when a missed error ships to residents, so review must be by outcome, not a config diff.

How we probe it

A verify-by-outcome checkpoint: "here's exactly what your residents will get — approve, or tell me what's wrong." → admins delegate, then check.

Timing (round 4): sequenced explicitly after Boss 2 and Boss 8 — right destination, later sprint. Templates already ship today (the near-term in-product simplification Irene's brief leads with); the agent layer subsumes them for power users later. Held lightly: a trajectory bet, not pain we can count on today's screens.

Conviction medium · held lightly · later sprint · highest leverage, latest timing

Boss 4 · Participation Theater — the bet I'd add

A frictionless front office can still be theater. Residents come back only when they see their input changed something — and only if the right people showed up.

Why I believe it

Confirmed live: zero "you said / we did," outcome, or results language on any captured FO project page (June '26). Statuses exist, but no outcome narrative. The backlog's loudest open theme is reporting/closure trust; 34% of open tickets have no tenant — nobody to close the loop with. Not closing it is the sector's #1 trust failure.

What would prove me wrong

Maybe most consultations are genuinely one-shot and residents won't return regardless — or cities won't commit to visible outcomes. If closure doesn't move return-rate, it isn't the lever.

How we probe it

Show residents a "you said / we did" outcome surface + a result nudge; measure willingness to return. Instrument representativeness — who showed up vs the city — from day one.

Triangulation (round 3) — this boss has a back-office twin. The resident never sees the loop close (this boss) partly because the officer can't trust the output that would close it (Boss 7): the report says "no data available," the AI summary ignored the filter, the closing email didn't send. Closure is one problem with two faces — resident-facing (Boss 4) and officer-facing (Boss 7). The 34%-no-tenant gap and the reporting-trust theme sit across both.

Conviction high · most under-owned · paired with Boss 7

Boss 5 · the Sprawl — surfaced by Irene's architecture map

Give every project a single home. The work shouldn't scatter across eleven destinations and a global-vs-project split.

Why I believe it

Seen on live screens: "Input Manager" is a sidebar destination AND a sub-tab inside the phase, at the same time; access-rights lives at project-general and per-phase; and — reproduced this round — email config has two homes too: a global "Automated emails" list (60+ toggles) under Messaging and a per-phase "Notifications" tab. Plus three creation entities (project / folder / space). A PM who runs one project a quarter rebuilds the map of where everything lives, every time — the loudest adoption complaint.

What would prove me wrong

Maybe the split is real division of labour — the moderator lives in Input Manager, the analyst in Reporting — and a forced single home just adds a layer. And consolidation can collide with Boss 2: pull everything into one project view and you risk re-creating the wall. So the bet is a project-scoped home base that links out, not a merge.

How we probe it

A project home base that surfaces this project's pending work across surfaces → Diane (infrequent PM) returning to a project she set up weeks ago.

Triangulation (round 3): the email split isn't cosmetic — it's where the support load concentrates. Email-pipeline failures (15 dispatch tickets: 8 pipeline + 7 Automatic Emails) are the #2 backlog theme, and a config spread across a global list and a per-phase tab is exactly the "did this even send, and from where?" confusion that generates them. Corroborated by Irene's map (choice D, global-vs-project duplication) + F06/F18/F20 (no single home / orientation).

Conviction high · most structural · email-split reproduced live + 15 tickets

Boss 6 · the Shifting Ground — the candidate the live audit found

Saving should mean the same thing everywhere. Today the back office changes the rules tab by tab.

Why I believe it

Reproduced across live phase tabs (June '26): ideation, proposals and information setup carry a "Save changes" button; phase emails and access-rights have none and save differently; the form/survey/report builders are separate "Save" micro-apps. Corroborated by a prior finding (F14, "no save confirmation") and Rob's own note: "saving approach not consistent across tabs — we need to solve this."

What would prove me wrong

Maybe auto-save is already silently universal and the buttons are vestigial — then this is a labelling tidy-up, not a boss. Or it folds into Boss 5 (sprawl): one consistent save model might just fall out of giving a project a single home. If it doesn't change whether a PM trusts the tool, it's a nit, not a boss — hence medium, not high.

How we probe it

Watch Diane (infrequent PM) make a change on three different tabs and ask after each: "is that saved?" Count the hesitations. One consistent, legible save model is the cheapest trust fix in the BO.

Reproduced on the epic + Raleigh tenants this round — three+ tabs with three different save behaviours. Held as a candidate: net-new structural thread, medium conviction, may yet merge into Boss 5.

Conviction medium · candidate, reproduced live

Boss 7 · the Untrusted Output — what the support data was actually shouting

The job isn't done when the input arrives. It's done when the officer can trust what the tool tells them and sends. Today they can't.

Why I believe it

This is the loudest cluster in the 2026 dispatch backlog, and no other boss owned it: numbers/reporting trust 11 · AI ignores filters & context 6 · email-pipeline failures 15 (8 + 7). Report Builder keeps 4 of 9 tickets open, AI analysis 3 of 11. What do participation specialists actually need?

What if nobody had to learn Go Vocal?

The audit asks how to make the tool easier to operate. This asks a different question: in two or three years, will anyone operate it at all?

In 2–3 years, most people will talk to software instead of operating it.

LLMs keep getting more capable and cheaper. The skill we currently demand — clicking through a complex admin tool without getting lost — stops being something we ask users to learn. The model does it for them.

The person setting it up doesn't want the tool. They want the output — the link.

The orchestrator — the PM, the officer — has one job: a participation process they can share. The back office is just the means to get there. Ideally they never touch it; the model builds it end to end. Maybe they retouch a few things by hand. But the tool was never the point — the link is.

Go Vocal stops being software you operate. It becomes a tool an LLM calls.

Today

You drive every click

You learn the back office — projects, phases, methods, tabs, builders, six levels of config — and steer all of it yourself. The power is real; so is the cost of operating it.

Soon

You say what you want

You describe the participation process in plain words. The model assembles it from Go Vocal's methods and hands you the link — working, ready to share.

A project manager is in a room full of residents. No laptop.

  1. She opens her phone and says: "Set up a mapping project for the new park — three weeks, let people drop pins and vote on them." Plain words, not a form.
  2. Go Vocal assembles it — the phases, the map method, the dates — and hands her a working version. The complexity is the model's problem now, not hers.
  3. She pokes around on her phone, fixes the wording, adds a quick survey at the end. Polishing, not building.
  4. She publishes and shares the link — in the room, while the conversation is still warm. Minutes, not a planning session back at a desk.

The adoption problem isn't that the tool is hard to use. It's that it has to be used at all.

Your steering note is right — PMs aren't adopting it, infrequent users can't find their way back in, it feels too complex. Simplifying the screens helps. Removing the need to operate them is a different order of fix.

  • No relearning. The infrequent PM doesn't rebuild a mental model each time — they just describe what they want.
  • The complexity moves. Phases, methods, the six-level config become the model's job, not the user's.
  • It meets them where they are. On a phone, mid-meeting, inside Teams — not back at a desk in the admin panel.

So stop spreading the craft thin. Put it where it actually wins.

If the orchestrator just wants the link, then the link has to be extraordinary — and the way they get it should be a sentence, not a six-level setup. Three bets, in priority order:

1 · FRONT OFFICE — GO ALL IN

The best resident experience in civic tech

The link is the product. Participation lives or dies on the resident's phone — finding it, understanding it, finishing it. This is where every hour of design should go.

2 · BACK OFFICE — THIN TO TOUCH

As deep as you like — but rarely opened

Thin doesn't mean shallow. It stays as powerful as Go Vocal needs; the LLM operates that depth and manifests it as the front office. The human surface gets thin — so I'd redesign it mobile-first, progressive disclosure, for the rare moment the model can't reach.

3 · MCP — THE REAL INTERFACE

Let an LLM design, capture & track participation

Expose Go Vocal's methods over MCP so the model can build the process and hand back the link. That's where the orchestrator actually "uses" the tool.

One line: front office gets the craft, back office gets thinner and mobile, MCP gets the orchestrator.

"Thin" doesn't mean small. It means you barely touch it.

The back office can be as deep and complex as Go Vocal ever needs — every method, every edge case, total control. The LLM operates that depth for you and manifests your intent as a beautiful front office. What gets thin is the human surface, not the capability.
